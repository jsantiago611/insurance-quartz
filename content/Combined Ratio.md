---
title: Combined Ratio
---
The [[Combined Ratio]] is the single most important number for determining if an insurer is any good at the actual insurance part of its business. It answers the question: "Are we selling our policies for more than they cost?"

It's just two numbers added together:

The [[Loss Ratio]]: The percentage of each premium dollar that gets eaten up by [[Claim]]s and the cost of adjusting them ([[Loss Adjustment Expenses (LAE)]]). This is the "cost of goods sold."

The [[Expense Ratio]]: The percentage that goes toward running the company itself—salaries, marketing, [[Agent]] commissions. This is the "overhead."

If you add them up and get less than 100%, you have an [[Underwriting Income]] profit. You are, on average, a good insurance company. If it's over 100%, you have an underwriting loss. You are losing money on every policy you write.

Here's the beautiful part: an insurer can have a combined ratio over 100% and still be wildly profitable because of [[Investment Income]] from the [[Float]]. This is why insurance is a weird business. You can be terrible at your primary product and still make a fortune. But if you want to be a great insurance company, you need a combined ratio consistently under 100.
